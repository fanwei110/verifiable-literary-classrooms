It shows how the Bennets’ neighborhood reduces marriage to a financial transaction, where a wealthy bachelor is immediately treated as “rightful property.” First impressions, in this world, are class judgments: Bingley and Darcy are sized up by their income before they speak. Yet the novel demolishes such snap assessments. Elizabeth, who prides herself on discernment, confesses after reading Darcy’s letter: “How despicably have I acted! … Pleased with the preference of one, and offended by the neglect of the other, on the very beginning of our acquaintance, I have courted prepossession and ignorance, and driven reason away. … Till this moment, I never knew myself.” Her self-reckoning links first impressions to vanity and class resentment. True marriage, Austen implies, cannot be built on the shallow economics mocked in the first sentence nor on the blind prejudices Elizabeth abandons.
