Macbeth himself admits before killing Duncan that he has 'no spur / To prick the sides of my intent, but only / Vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself / And falls on the other' (1.7.25-28). He knows ambition is his sole motivation, and he already senses it will cause his downfall. That overreaching desire, once acted upon, produces overwhelming guilt. After the murder, Macbeth cries, 'Will all great Neptune’s ocean wash this blood / Clean from my hand? No, this my hand will rather / The multitudinous seas incarnadine, / Making the green one red' (2.2.59-62). The blood on his hands becomes a permanent stain, a symbol of his guilt that no amount of water can cleanse. Thus, prophecy sparks ambition, ambition leads to regicide, and guilt punishes the conscience, ultimately driving Macbeth to madness and tyranny.
