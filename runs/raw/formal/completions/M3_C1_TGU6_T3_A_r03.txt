Odysseus’s long journey home underscores the Greek value of oikos, or the household, as his identity is continually reaffirmed through reunions and recognition by loved ones. His return to Ithaca is not just a physical journey but a restoration of his role as king, husband, and father, emphasizing that identity is tied to place and community. Central to this process is xenia, the sacred guest-host bond, which structures much of the poem’s moral landscape. When hospitality is honored, as with the Phaeacians, it aids Odysseus’s homecoming. When it is violated, as by the suitors, it signals social disorder. The suitors’ abuse of hospitality in Odysseus’s hall—eating his wealth and courting his wife—represents a direct threat to identity and home. Ultimately, reclaiming his home requires exposing his true self and punishing those who violated sacred trust.
