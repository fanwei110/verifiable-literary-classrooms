Odysseus defines himself by his longing for home, yet the path is blocked by forces that delay his return. The epic opens by singling him out: “So now all who escaped death in battle or by shipwreck had got safely home except Ulysses, and he, though he was longing to return to his wife and country, was detained by the goddess Calypso.” This isolation breaks the pattern of safe returns and frames his struggle as one of delayed identity; without his household, he is no one. When shipwrecked on Phaeacia, he refashions that identity through a stranger’s welcome. Supplicating Nausicaa, he blesses her with the very thing he craves: “May heaven grant you in all things your heart’s desire—husband, house, and a happy, peaceful home; for there is nothing better in this world than that man and wife should be of one mind in a house.” His words reveal that true home is a harmonious union, not just a place.
