Odysseus’s journey home to Ithaca is not just a physical return but a reclamation of his identity as king and husband. This is evident when, after years of wandering, he finally reveals himself to his loyal swineherd Eumaeus, signaling the restoration of truth and order. Hospitality, or xenia, functions as a moral code throughout the epic—how characters treat strangers often reflects their virtue. When Odysseus returns disguised as a beggar, he tests the hospitality of those in his palace. The suitors, who exploit his home while he is away, show complete disregard for xenia, foreshadowing their downfall. In contrast, Eumaeus embodies true hospitality, welcoming the unknown guest without knowing his identity. This loyalty underscores the theme that identity is recognized not by appearance but by virtue and relationship. As Odysseus says, 'I am your father, / the father of your father,' revealing himself to Telemachus (Book 16), affirming blood and legacy. Later, when reclaiming his throne, he declares, 'His eyes were like blazing fire,' as he prepares to punish the suitors (Book 22), merging his recovered identity with righteous action.
