God makes clear that humanity begins upright, endowed fully with the capacity to choose obedience: “Sufficient to have stood, though free to fall.” Temptation, then, operates on a will already free; it cannot force the fall, only persuade. Satan’s entire rebellion models this logic. His famous declaration, “Better to reign in Hell, than serve in Heaven,” is not a cry of victimhood but a deliberate, prideful choice to sever himself from beatitude. He recasts service as slavery, proving that rebellion germinates in the self-corruption of reason. Eve’s temptation replays this drama: the serpent’s argument merely presents a false good; her will alone consents. Thus the poem insists that blame lies not with God’s decree nor the tempter’s cunning, but with the creature’s misuse of liberty.
