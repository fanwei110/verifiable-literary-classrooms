First, the poem’s opening establishes Odysseus’s core identity: 'he was longing to return to his wife and country.' Despite war and wandering, his self is defined by the absence of home. Yet home isn’t just a place; as Odysseus later explains to Nausicaa, 'there is nothing better in this world than that man and wife should be of one mind in a house.' Here, homecoming means a harmonious marital union—a home that protects against enemies and gladdens friends. When the shipwrecked, naked stranger utters this, he relies utterly on the hospitality of strangers. Nausicaa grants him clothes and guidance, modeling the xenia that the suitors violate in Ithaca. Thus, identity is revealed through longing for home, true homecoming is a shared, peaceful household, and hospitality is the test that separates the civilized from the monstrous.
