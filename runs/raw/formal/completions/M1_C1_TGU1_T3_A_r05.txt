Hamlet’s infamous delay in avenging his father’s murder stems from his profound existential dread—he questions not only the morality of revenge but also the very meaning of life and death. His hesitation is not mere cowardice but a crisis of conscience, amplified by his awareness of mortality. The famous 'To be, or not to be' soliloquy reveals his torment: he contemplates suicide as an escape from life’s suffering but fears the unknown of death, illustrating how mortality paralyzes action. Additionally, Hamlet’s obsession with performance—both theatrical and personal—further complicates his resolve. He stages the 'Mousetrap' play to 'catch the conscience of the king,' yet his own performance as the 'antic disposition' blurs the line between truth and pretense, delaying his revenge. These themes intertwine, showing how overthinking and the fear of consequences can render even the most determined individuals inert.
