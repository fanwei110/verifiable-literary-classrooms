Homecoming (*nostos*) is not merely a physical return but a restoration of identity—Odysseus’s struggle to reclaim Ithaca mirrors his struggle to reclaim himself after years of wandering and divine captivity. The opening lines of the epic emphasize this longing: the narrator invokes the Muse to tell of the hero ‘who travelled far and wide after he had sacked the famous town of Troy’ and ‘was longing to return to his wife and country.’ This establishes homecoming as both a physical and emotional quest, one that defines Odysseus’s character and purpose.

Hospitality (*xenia*), meanwhile, is a sacred duty that tests and preserves identity. When Odysseus pleads with Nausicaa, he frames his request through the lens of shared humanity and divine favor: ‘May heaven grant you in all things your heart’s desire—husband, house, and a happy, peaceful home; for there is nothing better in this world than that man and wife should be of one mind in a house.’ Here, hospitality becomes a reciprocal exchange—Odysseus’s eloquence and humility earn him aid, while Nausicaa’s generosity reflects the Phaeacians’ reputation as a people ‘dear to the gods.’ Both moments underscore how identity is affirmed through connection: Odysseus’s return hinges on the kindness of strangers, just as his worth is proven by his ability to inspire it. These themes remind us that in *The Odyssey*, home is not just a place but a state of belonging, earned through perseverance and mutual respect.",
