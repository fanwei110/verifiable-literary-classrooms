Austen's opening irony—“It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune must be in want of a wife.”—satirizes a world where marriage is a class transaction. Mrs. Bennet's breathless news that Netherfield is let reveals that daughters are practically the ‘rightful property’ of any wealthy newcomer. Class anxieties reduce marriage to a hunt. Yet the novel's central lesson lies in the danger of first impressions formed through such a lens. Elizabeth's crucible moment arrives when she reads Darcy’s letter and exclaims: “How despicably have I acted! … Till this moment, I never knew myself.” She confesses that vanity, not love, drove her, that she ‘courted prepossession and ignorance’—favoring charming Wickham and scorning proud Darcy. Her self-reproach shows how class prejudice and wounded pride warp judgment. Only by humbling herself and revising her first impression can she recognize Darcy’s true worth. Austen thus argues that a lasting marriage must be founded not on fortune or flattery, but on mutual self-knowledge and the courage to overcome initial blindness.",
