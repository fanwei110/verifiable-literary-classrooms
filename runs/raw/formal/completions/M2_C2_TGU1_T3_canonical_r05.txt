Hamlet’s famous soliloquy, “To be, or not to be, that is the question,” opens a meditation on existence and the fear of what lies beyond death. His hesitation stems not from cowardice but from a mortal dread of the unknown—“the undiscovered country from whose bourn no traveller returns.” This inward delay contrasts with the outward action of theatre. Hamlet seizes on a traveling troupe to stage a murder, declaring, “The play’s the thing / Wherein I’ll catch the conscience of the King.” Here, performance becomes a weapon: by mirroring Claudius’s crime, Hamlet hopes to force the king’s guilt into the open. The play-within-a-play turns the court into an audience and the king into a performer, collapsing the boundary between reality and illusion. In this way, Shakespeare suggests that performance can bypass hesitation and mortality-driven anxiety—at least long enough to reveal truth.
