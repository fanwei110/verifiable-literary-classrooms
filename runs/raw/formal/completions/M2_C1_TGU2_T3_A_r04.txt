The witches' prophecy plants the seed of kingship, but it is Macbeth's 'vaulting ambition' that drives him to murder Duncan. He admits, 'I have no spur / To prick the sides of my intent, but only / Vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself / And falls on the other.' (1.7.25-28). Here, he confesses that ambition is his only motive, yet it's a dangerous force that can overreach and destroy. Once he kills, guilt immediately surfaces. After the murder, Macbeth stares at his bloody hands and despairs: 'Will all great Neptune's ocean wash this blood / Clean from my hand? No, this my hand will rather / The multitudinous seas incarnadine, / Making the green one red.' (2.2.60-63). The hyperbole shows his guilt is inescapable, staining the entire world. Thus, the prophecy ignites ambition, which in turn unleashes a guilt so vast that it psychologically damns him.
