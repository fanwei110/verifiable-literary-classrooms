Temptation is not an external force that overwhelms; it is a test that requires the exercise of free will. God explains that humanity was created “Sufficient to have stood, though free to fall,” establishing that obedience is meaningful only if disobedience is a genuine possibility. The Fall, then, results from a voluntary act of the will, not from a flaw in creation. Satan’s own rebellion models this dynamic. His famous declaration, “Better to reign in Hell, than serve in Heav’n,” is not the cry of a victim but the deliberate choice of a creature who has already fallen through pride. He redefines freedom as self-assertion rather than loving submission. The poem thus links temptation to an interior drama: the serpent’s words succeed because Eve’s will, though unfallen, can incline toward a lesser good. Rebellion is the outward shape of an inner capitulation.
