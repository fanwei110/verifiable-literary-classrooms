The epic opens by reminding us that Odysseus “was longing to return to his wife and country, was detained by the goddess Calypso.” His identity is defined by this aching desire for Ithaca—he is the man who refuses to forget home even when offered immortality. Yet home is not just a place; it’s a set of relationships. This becomes clear when the shipwrecked Odysseus, stripped of all status, supplicates the young Nausicaa. He doesn’t just beg for clothes; he invokes the ideal of a harmonious household: “for there is nothing better in this world than that man and wife should be of one mind in a house.” In doing so, he appeals to her understanding of what home means, subtly reminding her of her own future home. Her gracious response—granting him aid and a path to the city—demonstrates xenia, the sacred hospitality that makes homecoming possible.
