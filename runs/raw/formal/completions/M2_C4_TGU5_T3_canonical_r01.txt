  "answer": "In Paradise Lost, John Milton weaves temptation, free will, and rebellion into the very fabric of his epic. The poem opens by announcing its subject, “Of Mans First Disobedience, and the Fruit / Of that Forbidden Tree, whose mortal tast / Brought Death into the World, and all our woe.” Here, temptation is crystallized in the “Forbidden Tree” and its “mortal tast”; Adam and Eve’s free choice to eat the fruit is no helpless fall but a willed act of defiance. Free will itself is a divine gift, yet it makes disobedience possible. The consequence, “Death” and “all our woe,” shows that the cost of rebellion is catastrophic. \n\nSatan’s rebellion amplifies this theme. Banished to Hell, he declares, “Better to reign in Hell, then serve in Heav’n.” This is not passive temptation but active, self‑conscious revolt. Satan chooses sovereignty in torment over submission in bliss, twisting free will into a tool for eternal opposition. Both the human couple and Satan face temptation and exercise free will; both rebel. Yet Milton contrasts their rebellions: Satan’s is irrevocable pride, while Adam and Eve’s disobedience, though dire, will be healed by “one greater Man,” restoring the freedom they misused. The lesson of the poem is that free will is real, temptation tests it, and rebellion, whether angelic or human, carries profound consequences—but for humankind, redemption remains possible through grace.",
